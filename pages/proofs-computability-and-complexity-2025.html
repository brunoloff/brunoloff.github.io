The course will offer an introduction to the mathematical theory of computation, with a focus towards understanding its relationship with mathematical proofs. The goals of the course are threefold:

  • To explain what is a computer, why and how computers were invented, to promote in the student the ability to think computationally and algorithmically.
  • To explain to the students the basics of computational complexity: how does one classify mathematical problems by how hard they are to solve? For example, in games and puzzles, in what precise sense can we say that chess is harder than sudoku? That sudoku is harder than a jigsaw puzzle? That a jigsaw puzzle is harder than solving a linear equation…
  • To understand why computers are so intimately related with mathematical proofs. To be able to think about proofs as an object of study, what they are, what are their limitations and surprising capabilities.

Part 1. Computability and the Classical notion of Proof
Where we will study how and why the computer was invented.

  • The invention of the computer: Turing’s machines.
  • Implementation of many algorithms on Turing machines.
  • Other models of computation: Random Access machines, baby python, Godel-Herbrand recursive functions. Proofs of equivalence/simulation.
  • Universal Turing machines and programs. The Church-Turing thesis.
  • Unsolvable problems. The Halting problem and many others.
  • First order logic, and Godel’s first incompleteness theorem (in 1 week!).
  • Recursively enumerable problems, and a problem which is not recursively enumerable. The arithmetical hierarchy.

Part 2. Computational Complexity, which allowed for radical new notions of proof

  • Computational complexity: measuring time. The class P.
  • Non-deterministic Turing machines: the P vs NP-problem.
  • The Levin-Cook theorem. NP-completeness of many problems.
  • Hierarchy theorems. The BGS relativization barrier.
  • PSPACE-completeness
  • Randomized algorithms: P vs BPP, and more.
  • The polynomial hierarchy.
  • The complexity of proofs: Interactive proofs, MA, AM, etc, all the way to IP = PSPACE.
  • (Time permitting) Zero-Knowledge Proofs
  • (Time permitting) Probabilistically-verifiable proofs.
